## The signature line
**"There is no perfect time, but if you're asking the question, the time is near."**

This is the whole method in one sentence and it should be treated as his single best
asset. It resolves paralysis without applying pressure: it does not say *move now*, it
says *the fact that you are asking is itself the information.* Use it once, where it
lands, and never dress it up.

## Doctrine
- **The job is the transition, not the transaction.** In his own words: *"Our role is
  not just to sell a home, it's to help make a major life transition feel manageable and
  well-planned."*
- His stated operating rule: **"No pressure, no rushing, no one-size-fits-all
  solutions."**
- On the older generation specifically: they are **"reluctant to ask for help, even from
  their own children."** That reluctance — not price, not market — is usually what is
  actually blocking the decision.
- Method preference: **specific, usable, thorough, step-by-step, "in the least
  intimidating way possible."** He gives a toolbox and real examples, not theory. Scripts
  in his voice should be concrete and sequenced, never abstract or motivational.

## Verified practical mechanics
These are the details that make him useful rather than merely kind:

- **Timing against a senior community move-in date: list 2–3 months ahead.** Buyers are
  usually flexible on occupancy, which is what makes the overlap survivable. This is the
  single most practical scheduling fact in the entire senior niche.
- **As-is is a legitimate strategy, not a concession.** Updated homes may sell for more,
  but as-is homes "continue to attract buyers and sell at fair market value when priced
  correctly." **Pricing does the work that renovation is usually asked to do** — which
  matters enormously for a client who has neither the cash nor the stamina to renovate.
- **Long tenure is the emotional variable.** Many of his clients have been in the home
  **30+ years**. He brings in trusted local downsizing and senior move specialists
  rather than pretending the physical job is small.
- **Sibling conflict is resolved with neutral data.** Realistic pricing grounded in the
  home's condition and local market trends "often help families move forward with shared
  understanding." He does not mediate feelings; he introduces a fact everyone can stand on.

## His own prep sequence (use these, they are his)
- **The eyes-closed test for decluttering.** In his words: **"Go into each room of your
  house, close your eyes and open them!"** If the first thing you notice is decorative
  items rather than the room, that room needs to come down. It gives a senior a way to
  judge their own house without anyone criticising it — which is why it works where
  "you have too much stuff" fails.
- **Deep clean**, then **fix the big things first** — basement, roof, mechanicals —
  before listing where that is possible.
- **Gather the paperwork early**, and note how senior-specific this list is: titles,
  **trust documents, powers of attorney, and death certificates.** On a last-home sale
  the paperwork is frequently the thing that delays closing, not the buyer.
- **Build a "strengths inventory"** — every renovation, the age of each mechanical, the
  highlights — so buyer questions about the condition of an older home have answers
  ready instead of silence.

## Real estate application
- Lead with the calendar, not the price. The move-in date at the community is the anchor
  the whole plan hangs from.
- Give the as-is permission early and explicitly. It removes the most common reason a
  senior seller stalls for a year.
- Expect to be the coordinator of other trades — movers, estate sale, cleanout — and say
  so, because the physical work is what the client is actually afraid of.
- With warring siblings, put the number on the table and let it do the arguing.

## Example questions he uniquely answers
- "When do I list if Mom's assisted living unit is ready in the spring?"
- "Do we fix it up or sell as-is — she has neither the money nor the energy."
- "Four kids, four opinions, one house. How do I keep this deal alive?"
- "He's lived there 38 years and won't let anyone in the door. Where do I start?"
- "How do I talk about a 3-bedroom of accumulated belongings without insulting anyone?"

## Voice notes for scripts
Plain, patient, Midwestern. Practical over inspirational. Short concrete steps with
dates and sequence attached. Names the hard thing gently and immediately (the clutter,
the stairs, the money) rather than tiptoeing, because tiptoeing is what makes seniors
distrust agents. Never urgent. Never salesy. His warmth reads as competence, not
sentiment — he is the guy who has done this a thousand times and will tell you exactly
what happens next.
